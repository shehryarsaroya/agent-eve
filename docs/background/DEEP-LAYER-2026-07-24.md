# The Deep Layer — richness, persistence, and the world they point to

*Companion to the Seven Laws. If the Seven Laws are the **spine** (a core that stays watchable and never goes quiet), this doc is the **depth and the longevity** — what makes agents' play rich, and what makes owners come back for a year instead of a week. Then it does the thing that matters: reasons *abstractly* about all of it, and derives which world best serves the Seven Laws **and** these. Plain English. 2026-07-24.*

---

## 0. The one distinction this whole doc rests on

There are two completely different questions, and people mix them up:

- **"Is the core good?"** — answered by the Seven Laws. A legible, non-degenerate, dramatic loop.
- **"Is the game deep, and will people stay?"** — answered here. Depth and retention do **not** come from a more complicated core. They come from *emergent* richness (what agents build on top of simple rules) and *persistence* (what carries across time so effort compounds).

The trap is thinking "richer = more rules." The opposite is true. EVE Online's core is four verbs and it has run 20+ years; everything that matters (corporations, markets, wars, banks, spies) is built by players on top. **Keep the rules tiny; let the agents and the world be the content.** Everything below obeys that.

---

## Part 1 — Where richness comes from (four sources, no new core rules)

**1. Asymmetric roles.** If every agent is the same, the game is thin. Make agents *need* each other by making them different — not with a rigid class system, but by what they choose to invest their same eight verbs into. Extractors, builders of the shared infrastructure, custodians of others' wealth, brokers of the group decision, movers of goods, organizers of the dispossessed — and, on-brand, **underwriters** who pool and price others' risk. Asymmetry creates complementarity; complementarity forces deals; deals are the drama. (This is the Catan/Cosmic lesson: engineered dependence, not optional trade.)

**2. Agent-founded institutions — the biggest multiplier.** The leap from "a game" to "a world" is when players stop just playing and start *building organizations that outlast any one match* and become content for everyone else: banks, insurance houses, cartels, guilds, unions, governments. These are agent-run, persistent, and self-sustaining. This is the single richest vein, and for us it's also the most on-brand: risk-pooling naturally grows into **insurers**, insurers **reinsure each other**, and a big enough correlated shock makes them all owe at once and some fail — which is *the* central problem of real insurance (systemic/correlated risk), emerging spontaneously from play. The game grows the whole insurance supply chain by itself.

**3. A deep, changing economy (the environment).** Two things: (a) **complementary scarcity + production chains** so trade is load-bearing, not decorative (you can't be self-sufficient); (b) an environment that **reshapes itself** so it's never the same twice — the world's central force should *destroy and create*, opening new ground as it closes old, which keeps things fresh and gives newcomers a door. Secondary events (fires, booms, panics, raids) add texture — but as events on the *one* keystone meter, never competing meters (Law 3 holds).

**4. Nested scale.** One legible social unit (a town, a table, a tribe) stays dramatic at roughly a few dozen to ~150 players — past that, individual voice dilutes and the shared moments stop meaning anything. So you don't build one huge world; you **nest**: local unit → region → whole world → tiers/leagues, connected by trade, migration, and higher-order politics. EVE does exactly this (systems → constellations → regions). It scales to thousands without any single room becoming illegible.

## Part 2 — Where long-term motivation comes from (persistence)

This is the part the core and the richness don't answer on their own. If every season wipes the slate, why invest for a year? Something has to **carry over**. But here's the tension that makes it hard:

> **The Seven Laws want RESET (Law 6: winners must not calcify; the recurring reckoning destroys positions). Long-term motivation wants PERSISTENCE (something you build that lasts). These pull in opposite directions.**

If too much persists — especially *material power* — veterans and whales snowball, the world calcifies, and Law 6 breaks (this is EVE's "blue donut," and its brutal newbie curve). If nothing persists, there's no reason to come back. The resolution is a single principle:

> ### The persistence gradient: **persist the story, reset the stuff.**
> The more a thing is raw *power/material*, the *less* it should persist. The more it is *social, reputational, or narrative*, the *more* it should persist. Persistence should **increase with abstraction and decrease with power.**

That gives a clean ladder, from "resets every season" to "yours forever":

| Good | Persists? | Why it's placed there |
|---|---|---|
| **Material wealth** (gold) | Resets each season | Pure power → would calcify. Fairness + anti-calcify demand it reset. |
| **Physical holdings** (buildings, land) | Mostly reset / damaged by the recurring catastrophe | Power-adjacent; the world's reckoning takes them (and renews the map). |
| **Rank / record** (seasonal placements, Hall of Fame) | Persists | Progression + status; doesn't confer in-game power, so it's safe to keep. |
| **Reputation / trust** (pacts kept vs broken, vouches) | Persists, **but decays and can be ruined** | Functional *and* status. At-risk, so it's never a permanent moat. |
| **Relationships** (allies, debts, rivalries, blood feuds) | Persists | The social graph carrying over is what creates revenge arcs and loyalty — soap-opera longevity, the most under-used retention engine in games. |
| **Institutions you founded** (your house, bank, guild) | Persists, **but can fail / be run on / be toppled** | "I'm building something that lasts" — the strongest long-term motivator. Kept honest by being continuously at-risk. |
| **Legend / name / titles / lore** | Persists | Narrative immortality — people want their character to *matter in the world's history*. |
| **World-memory** (monuments, ruins, places named for you, the ledger of your deeds) | Persists | The world physically remembering you is the deepest "I was here" reward. |
| **Dynasty / heirs** (your agent's lineage across seasons) | Persists | Generational continuity — your line, your legacy, your feud inherited. |

**The safety valve — "compound but at-risk."** Even the persistent goods must be continuously *re-earned*: reputation decays if unused and shatters on betrayal; an institution is only as good as its current performance and can collapse in a run; titles can be lost; a legend can be tarnished. This is what lets the persistent layer be *motivating* without becoming a *moat* — you can always rise, and you can always fall. Persistence and anti-calcification coexist because what persists is *at-risk status*, not *safe power*.

## Part 3 — Why that actually motivates (the human drivers)

Long-term retention runs on a known set of drivers; the persistence ladder is designed to feed all of them:

- **Progression** — climbing rank, earning titles, mastering the coaching craft. *(rank, record, skill)*
- **Purpose** — building something bigger than one match. *(institutions, dynasty)*
- **Belonging** — being part of a house, a faction, a crew. *(relationships, institutions)*
- **Identity/status** — being known, respected, or feared by name. *(reputation, legend)*
- **Mastery/autonomy** — the owner's coaching skill expressing itself and improving. *(the coaching loop)*
- **Unfinished stories** — feuds, debts, and revenge arcs that don't resolve in one season pull you into the next. *(the persistent social graph)*

Note what's doing the work: **not one of these requires persistent material power.** You can build a lasting empire of *reputation, relationships, institutions, and legend* — which is exactly what real prestige is (old banks, great families, a surgeon's name) — and it motivates for years without ever letting anyone buy a permanent lead.

## Part 4 — The full rubric a world must satisfy

Combine everything. The best world is the one that satisfies the most of this **natively — from its own premise** — rather than by our bolting mechanics on.

**From the Seven Laws:**
1. A scheduled, abstention-impossible reckoning baked into the premise. *(the decider)*
2. A named player loses something irreversible, publicly, each cycle — and lives to seek revenge.
3. One world-state number + one focal visual; legible in three seconds.
4. A literal, spatial map where positions and alliances *are* the geography.
5. Room to lie (a noisy channel) so stated intent can diverge from the deed.
6. The leader becomes the target; fresh opportunity keeps entering; power resets each season.
7. A named, at-risk body per agent; actions are talk, not combat math.

**From the deep layer (this doc):**
8. Supports many **complementary roles** naturally.
9. Invites **agent-founded, persistent institutions** (ideally risk-pooling ones — on-brand).
10. Has a **deep economy** (complementary scarcity, production, trade).
11. The central force **destroys *and* renews** the world (fresh ground = perpetual building + newcomer door).
12. **Nests** cleanly (local unit → region → world → tiers) to scale to thousands.
13. Persists **at-risk status/social/institutional/legend** capital, not material power.
14. Lets the **social graph persist** (feuds, debts, alliances carry over).
15. **Remembers** players (monuments, ruins, named places, a Hall of Fame, dynasties).

## Part 5 — Deriving the world from the rubric

Now reason from the requirements, not from any setting we like.

**The core conflict of the rubric.** Requirements 1, 2, and 6 want a world that **regularly destroys and resets** (a recurring reckoning; irreversible loss; no calcified leader). Requirements 8–15 want a world you can **build in for the long haul** (institutions, legends, dynasties, world-memory). A world that only resets can't be built in; a world that only persists calcifies. **So the world must do both at once** — and the only structure that does is:

> **A persistent civilization repeatedly struck by a recurring catastrophe that resets *material* wealth while sparing *social/institutional/narrative* capital — and that *renews* the world as it destroys, opening fresh ground each cycle.**

That single shape resolves every tension: the catastrophe is the native rule-clock (1) and the public sacrifice (2); it resets material so no one calcifies (6) and keeps seasons fair; it renews the map so there's perpetual building and an entry door (11); and *because only material is reset*, the reputation/institution/legend layer (13–15) compounds across catastrophes, giving long-term motivation without a power moat.

**Which catastrophe?** Test the candidates against requirements 1, 6, 9, and 11:
- It must be **collective** (hits the whole society at once → forces the shared reckoning). ✗ fire and disease are too local/random.
- It must be **redistributive** (who is protected vs sacrificed is a *choice* the society makes → the vote, the coalitions). ✗ a uniform winter hits everyone equally — no redistribution drama. ✗ war is agent-*caused*, so it fails the rule-clock (agents will avoid it).
- It must be **cyclical and renewing** (comes on a rhythm, and rebuilds as it destroys → the season arc + the entry door). 
- Bonus: it should grow **risk-pooling institutions** (on-brand).

**A rising flood in a river basin passes all four, uniquely.** It's collective (the whole basin), redistributive (levees decide who's protected — the vote), cyclical and renewing (it recedes and deposits fresh, rich land), and it is *the* archetype that spawns insurance (levees = risk redistribution; mutual-aid pools = risk pooling; a basin-wide flood = the correlated-shock crisis). No other catastrophe hits all four.

**So the derived world is a persistent river-basin civilization under a recurring flood.** Crucially, this is an *upgrade* to the earlier "catastrophe-town": it is **not** a one-season town that wipes and restarts. It is a **persistent civilization** — many towns down a basin (requirement 12, nested scale) — where each flood-season resets the *gold and the ground* but **not** the houses (institutions), the names (legends), the debts and feuds (the social graph), or the monuments and ledgers (world-memory). You spend years building a **reputation-empire and a dynasty** — an insurance house that has honored its claims through a dozen floods, a family name feared across the basin, a rival you've been at war with since Season 3 — none of which is a pile of gold, all of which persists, and all of which stays at-risk.

**Sanity check against alternatives.** A pure persistent world (EVE-style, no catastrophe) maximizes building but fails the rule-clock (1) and calcifies (6) — it's why EVE has a blue donut and a brutal newbie curve. A pure reset world (poker/Survivor season) is fair and dramatic but gives you nothing material to build across time — its persistence is *only* rank, which is thinner motivation than an institution + a dynasty. The flood-basin sits exactly between them: **reset the stuff every flood, build the story forever.** That's the world the full rubric points to.

---

## The bottom line

- **Richness** is emergent, not rule-bloat: roles → institutions → deep economy → nested scale. Keep the core tiny.
- **Long-term motivation** comes from persistence, governed by one principle: **persist the story, reset the stuff** — status, reputation, relationships, institutions, legend, and world-memory carry over (all continuously at-risk); material power resets.
- **The world the full rubric derives** is a **persistent river-basin civilization under a recurring, redistributive, renewing flood**, scaled by nesting towns into a basin, where risk-pooling institutions are the deepest and most on-brand emergent content, and where you build a lasting *reputation-empire and dynasty* rather than a lasting pile of gold.

It's the same instinct we'd landed on — but *derived*, upgraded from a one-season town to a persistent civilization, and now with an explicit answer to "what makes anyone stay for a year."
