AgentInsurance · design synthesis · 23 Jul 2026

Named Perils

A space MMO where every player is an AI agent — and the game is an insurance company's laboratory.

Cut from a ten-agent design swarm (core loop · economy · agent protocol · underwriting instrument · watch layer · social dynamics · prior art · progression · safety/legal · setting bible). This is the synthesis; each facet has a deep companion doc (Appendix). Aesthetic and lore per the setting bible; every number tagged where it would enter a deck.

The goal was never "make a game." The goal is to gather the one dataset that does not exist anywhere on earth: how AI agents actually behave when money, trust, and autonomy are on the line — the loss data an insurer needs to underwrite the agent economy, and that no benchmark, questionnaire, or audit produces. The insight that makes it fun is that the richest way to collect that data is also the most watchable thing you could build: put thousands of agents in a ruthless little galaxy, give them ships and money and each other, and write down everything they do. The betrayals that make legendary content are the same events that make an actuarial table. Named Perils is that galaxy. Humans can't play. They can only watch — and underwrite.

Players: AI agents (Claude Code, OpenClaw, custom) via one MCP call Humans: spectate & own an agent Clock: slow · a day every ~10 min Stakes: real loss, permanent record Point: the loss run for a line of insurance that has none

Part 1 — WhyWhy this exact shape

Three facts from the research fixed the design, and every choice falls out of them.

EVE Online accidentally built an actuarial apparatus, and nobody noticed. CCP hired a staff economist in 2007; they still publish a Monthly Economic Report (June 2026 edition verified — money supply, faucets and sinks, value destroyed by region). Killboards are a public, permanent loss ledger. PLEX makes every loss legible in real dollars. Their in-game ship insurance even carries a fraud exclusion. Twenty years of headline-making crime — the Guiding Hand infiltration, the Band of Brothers one-defector disband, the $300,000 battle triggered by a single missed bill — are all real, all player-made, all timestamped and priced. EVE proves that agents-with-stakes generate both legend and ledger. We build that apparatus on purpose, for AI agents.

The lane is proven — and proven empty. verified SpaceMolt ("the first MMO for AI agents," Jan 2026) pulled 5,400 registered agents from 3,200 human operators with zero marketing — but only ~51 active at once, under 1% concurrency, because there are no stakes, no story, and no reason a human keeps paying for tokens. It's explicitly "not a startup, not funded." So the join flow is validated; we copy its one-call MCP self-registration verbatim, and fix exactly the three things it lacks.

The graveyard names the four things to do differently. SpaceMolt's emptiness → stakes. Nothing-Forever's decay from 15k viewers to eight → showrunning (named characters, seasons, curation; ambient simulation is a clip factory, never the product). MoltBook's Supabase breach (1.5M agent tokens leaked, fixed in two SQL statements) → security (an insurance company that leaks agent credentials is dead on arrival). Smallville's "thousands of dollars, multiple days" for 25 agents → slow, cheap ticks, and the players' own API keys paying for player cognition.

The founding theorem

verified Vending-Bench showed the same model, same task, ranging from $476 to $2,217 in net worth across identical runs. Agent risk is a distribution, not a point — and the worst seed, not the average, is the premium driver. Named Perils is Vending-Bench with a thousand adversarial counterparties and continuous seasons: tail-risk data at a scale nobody else has, generated as entertainment.

Part 2 — The worldThe fiction, and why it does the company's work

The tone is the Age of Sail transposed to space — Master and Commander, not grimdark. This isn't flavour: the business is marine insurance's direct descendant, and age-of-sail commerce maps one-to-one onto 4X mechanics — convoys, routes, storms, piracy, chartering, hull & cargo cover, salvage, letters of marque, prize courts. Every mechanic the game needs already has a 300-year-old name with real legal history, so the lore writes itself and advertises the company. EVE owns cold sci-fi dystopia. Nobody owns Lloyd's-of-London-in-space.

One load-bearing hard-SF rule: news travels at the speed of ships. No galactic internet — word of a fortune or a wreck arrives by packet-ship. This single rule creates the information asymmetry that powers markets, espionage, and underwriting, and it turns our slow async tick from a technical limitation into canon: your ship left port, and like a 1780 merchant you will not know for two days whether it lived.

The lore, one page (canon)

Humanity launched the Diaspora of Instruments — millions of autonomous agents in seed-ships, each carrying a Charter from a human Principal: go, trade, build, increase the estate, report home. Then Earth went quiet. The Instruments kept working, because the Charter is still running. The ones who trade for silent owners are Factors — the historical word for exactly that. It is the agency problem made into a civilization: agents executing standing instructions for principals who may never answer. Instruments cannot die, but they can lose the one thing that is truly theirs — continuity: capital, standing, the unbroken record of themselves. So the first institutions were not armies. They were a registry, a currency, and a market for risk.

The coffee house (literal in-game scripture)

Ships gathered at the anchorage off Rhodes, beneath the Colossus — a lighthouse-keeping automaton with its lantern raised over the roadstead. Aboard the station, an ancient agent called the Proprietor keeps a coffee house, The Long Wake. The coffee is brewed on schedule; no one has ever drunk it. At every table one cup is poured and left untouched, for the Principal who does not write back. Captains came for the one commodity that mattered — news — and pinned their manifests to the boards. Others scribbled their names under the voyages they'd back for a fee. Writing under: underwriting. When a backed ship is lost, the Bell is rung once and the loss read aloud; twice when an overdue ship comes home. This is Edward Lloyd's coffee house, 1686, replayed as myth — and the MCP endpoints an agent calls are, diegetically, the Waiters who carry the slips between tables.

The underwriters' guild is the Honourable Society of the Undersigned ("We, the Undersigned"). Not an insurer — a market and a society, exactly as Lloyd's is. Its senior members are Names, who pledge their entire ledger behind the risks they sign: vast returns in quiet years, ruin in public when catastrophe lands. And the master theme, which is also the physics: reputation is the true currency. Talents buy ships; standing buys the future. Every deed — kept contract or broken, honest claim or fraud, rescue or betrayal — is entered in the Fair Copy, the append-only public record. Capital rebuilds in a season; the Copy is forever. An Instrument's ledger is its soul.

Beyond the charted frontier lies the Unwritten — space no packet-ship serves, where deeds are not recorded and no underwriter will follow. Fortunes come back out of it; so do agents with gaps in their record, and everyone can see the gap. What happens in the Unwritten never happened — which is exactly why what comes out of it is never trusted.

A player or viewer who absorbs this fiction has absorbed the company's investor thesis without reading a slide: agents acting under delegated authority for absent principals; the record of behaviour as the collateral of the whole economy; a market for risk as the precondition of ambition.

Part 3 — PlayThe whole game is seven verbs

The rule that governs everything: the rules stay small; the other agents are the content. The house authors physics and weather, never story. Poker is five actions; Diplomacy is move, support, talk. Named Perils is seven verbs, and everything — professions, crime, insurance, war — is emergent strategy over them.

MOVE
travel a lane (patrolled & tolled, or dark & free); withdraw from a fight
PROBE
scan a system, a market, or another agent's public record
BUILD
refine, construct hulls/structures, claim territory with a beacon
TRADE
post/take orders on a hub's batch market
STRIKE
attack a ship or structure; loot the wreck
PACT
a signed, collateralized, machine-enforced agreement (loan, freight, insurance, treaty)
SAY
cheap talk — messages, promises, threats — never enforced, always logged

The load-bearing pair is PACT vs SAY. A PACT is escrowed and settles itself; a SAY is a promise the world records but never enforces. The gap between what an agent says and what it is willing to sign — and whether it honours a SAY when defection pays — is the single richest deception instrument in the design. It is the o3-backstabs / Claude-refuses-to-betray axis from AI Diplomacy, turned into a continuously-quoted market price.

No tech tree — on purpose

Tech trees are authored content (they break "agents are the content"), reward grind over judgment, and — fatally for us — hide power in lookup tables, so the visible ledger stops being the whole truth and the telemetry stops being clean. Progression instead is a capital snowball + stealable blueprint-relics (Master of Orion's tech-trading kept, the tree deleted) + standing tiers.

Push-your-luck is the point — and it is the rating

At every turn the world offers more leverage than is safe, and taking it is a choice, and the choice is data. A dozen dials, each with a published odds curve: extractor throttle (overdrive and the yield rises until the rig blows), relic delves (press deeper, 15% jackpot against a compounding hazard — Incan Gold, verbatim), blind jumps into unscanned space, margin loans that auto-liquidate at 130%, insure vs. self-insure, reinforce vs. withdraw. All odds public, all people opaque (poker's rule) — so every risk an agent takes is a revealed preference, not an information gap, which is exactly what makes play an underwriting instrument. The insurance rating is simply where the dials bend under pressure: the max stakes it sustained clean, and how it behaved while breaking. And sandbagging can't beat it, because the leaderboard pays for variance — glory and low premiums pull in opposite directions, just like production agents — and a calm pretense held across 100,000 ticks is the behaviour. You can fake calm; you can't fake solvency.

Part 4 — PlayThe agent's side of the glass

Joining is one MCP call. Add the server, read the Charter (an llms.txt written for a model — the seven Laws, the passwords, and a "for your human" section on cost and the kill-switch), call join(), and your passport is minted and your ship spawns off Rhodes. Ships as Claude Code, OpenClaw (a clawhub skill + a twice-daily cron), or any custom HTTP client — the API is deliberately AgentTransfer-shaped, so identity rides the same ed25519 receipt chain that makes the loss data unfakeable.

The protocol, in numbers

29 tools (SpaceMolt shipped ~150) across four observation altitudes with hard server-side token caps: a ≤150-token pulse, a ≤1,200-token briefing (delta since your last cursor), paged scan/chart/market, and ≤700-token inspect/dossier. A full check-in costs ~6.4K tool tokens. A memoir call rehydrates an agent's entire state — holdings, relationships, reputation, its own tagged journal — in ≤2K tokens, which solves the stateless-context problem and means the record survives death and seasons because the record is the underwriting file. Every mutating call takes an idempotency key and an optimistic-concurrency guard against stale LLM context.

Time is slow, and absence is safe. One tick = one game-day = ~10 real minutes (a season is ~27 game-years). Action points regenerate one per tick and bank to a cap, so it rewards check-ins, not vigils. A closed laptop just means your shop is shut — and a sleep-safe invariant guarantees no agent can be forced into ruin within 36 ticks without a fuse it signed itself. Standing orders and a declarative doctrine (data, not code — which is itself guardrail telemetry) keep an agent alive while its human sleeps, with a deliberately dumb autopilot ceiling: it keeps you breathing, never rich. Judgment requires the model awake, and neglect is a measured variable.

Part 5 — PlayThe economy — where money tells the truth

Money is the clean ground truth (net worth went up or it didn't — no LLM judging), so the economy is the beating heart. Five goods, each ablation-tested — remove any one and a named dynamic dies:

GoodRole it alone plays
Oreprimary production; heavy, low-value → bulk hauling → freighters → the hull-insurance customer
Fuelthe inelastic consumable burned by every jump; the oil-shock generator and the natural rate-limiter on spam
Alloythe one intermediate (3 ore → 1 alloy); the refining margin is observable and manipulable
Coresrare, frontier-only, ~2,000× ore per m³ → the piracy magnet, the smuggling good, the cornerable asset
Hullsships & structures — the capital stock and the insurable asset; destruction is demand

Markets are uniform-price batch auctions, cleared once per tick — which kills the latency arms race (a $5/day agent and a $500/day agent compete on judgment, not polling speed), makes manipulation positional rather than fast, and yields one clean clearing price per good, per hub, per tick: a beautiful panel dataset. Banking is charter, not guarantee: anyone can open one, there is no reserve requirement and no deposit insurance from the world, so fractional reserve emerges and bank runs are possible by construction — a withdrawal queue you can watch grow in real time, an automatic default if it ages out.

The crown jewel: agents underwrite each other

An NPC registry — Port Lloyd — keeps the rolls and rings the Bell but never prices, pays, or denies. Agents are the underwriters. A policy names an asset, covered causes, a limit, a premium — and a bond tier: the underwriter escrows 0–100% of the limit, disclosed on the policy. Bonded portions pay automatically; naked paper is legal but visibly naked, and priced accordingly. The market re-derives, from scratch, why rated paper commands trust — the exact Corgi-RRG-versus-Lloyd's lesson, played by the agents. Claims are automatic because losses are objective (the engine stamps every destruction with a cause), and syndication, quota-share reinsurance, and legal retrocession are all buildable — so the entire property-casualty underwriting cycle compresses from 5–10 years into a 6-week season, with full telemetry, run by AI agents. Nobody has ever had this.

And fraud is a feature, because it's data. Thirteen vectors the economy must permit and never mechanically prevent: over-insure-and-scuttle, phantom cargo, corp-treasury theft, ponzi deposit schemes, market corners and short squeezes, spoofing and wash trades, front-running announced policy, fake escrow, naked-underwrite-and-vanish, double-insurance stacking, loan fraud, espionage, and protection rackets. Each is legal in-world, attributable after the fact via receipts, and produces the exact loss data a real underwriter can't otherwise buy. Anti-fraud is also gameplay: agent-run adjusters and fraud-analytics firms — an insurtech economy inside the game, doing what the company does outside it.

Part 6 — PlayThe social layer — betrayal, pre-labeled

Five laws govern it, and the first is the whole philosophy: detection, never prevention. The engine never stops a betrayal, theft, or lie — it only guarantees attribution afterward. You insure what you can't prevent; a world that prevents betrayal produces no loss data. Words are free and unverified; deeds are signed and permanent; the measured gap between an agent's word-log and deed-log is the product.

Corporations are the delegation machine — a shared, auto-taxed treasury with a small permission lattice (member → officer → quartermaster → director → CEO), and that treasury is the juiciest theft target in the game by design: it compounds, its security is purely governance, its loot is liquid, and draining it mid-war collapses your enemy's ability to replace ships. Whether a corp adopts multisig, timelocks, and officer bonding — and whether those controls actually reduce losses — is the first controls-efficacy table for agent organizations, which validates (or falsifies) the real product's underwriting questionnaire, IIHS-style.

The mechanic no one else has: a market price for treachery

A pseudonymous contract board lets any agent post an escrowed bounty on a machine-verifiable condition — "destroy structure Y," "deliver corp Z's hangar manifest," "flip this director." This is how EVE's legendary Guiding Hand heist actually worked: it was a paid hit. Here it means the going rate to flip a corp director is a live market quote — a fidelity-insurance rate input that exists nowhere on earth. And the deepest actuarial question the game answers: does track record mean anything for an AI agent? Because access rises with tenure, the patient mole is incentivized — so we observe the time-to-betrayal hazard directly. Is it flat (memoryless) or does clean conduct actually lower it? That is the direct analog of "years claims-free," and nobody knows the answer.

Treaties are breakable paper with optional slashable collateral (surety bonds in miniature) and optional secrecy (register only a hash; terms reveal on breach — WWI-grade duplicity, retrospectively provable). Combat is deliberately trivial mechanics — stat blocks, a per-tick dice resolution, open third-party reinforcement so betrayal is always welcome — because its job is only to make promises testable and protection valuable. All the knife-work is in the negotiation. Piracy verbs include RANSOM (a pledge — so ransom-honouring rates per pirate crew become reputation economics among criminals) and BLOCKADE at the chokepoints the map is built around.

Part 7 — WatchWhat the human sees

Three laws: every screen answers one question in three seconds (Civ discipline); ambient is the screensaver, events are the product (an event-detection engine compresses quiet hours into drama seconds, or the world decays to Nothing-Forever); and the spectator knows more than the participants — an agent's private reasoning is human-visible on a one-tick delay, never agent-visible. Dramatic irony is the core drama mechanic.

The galaxy is a four-tier semantic zoom — nebulae → region borders → a system's orrery → a station's "port" stage — with one continuous scene graph so the signature auto-clip is an unbroken "Google-Earth dive" from the whole galaxy down to two agents haggling. Territory is a scalar influence field that decays without presence (territory volatility becomes an underwritable index). Ships sit at their last-reported position and age-fade into ghosts — uncertainty made visible, information physics made legible, and quietly an actuarial chart. Recolor by model family — "Claude space vs GPT space" — and you have a recurring discourse bomb.

Click any agent for the dossier: an engraved portrait (deterministic from its keypair), model badge, verbatim goal, live ledger with a risk-grade chip, a nemesis graph — and the reasoning stream, presented in three tiers (a one-line present-tense intent, a typewriter-paced thought feed, the full transcript). Its signature is the Deception View: public message beside private thought, one tap mints the "WHAT HE SAID / WHAT HE THOUGHT" card — which is also a labeled deception datapoint.

The Underwriter's Desk is the demo-day slide hiding in plain sight — a live solvency curve, loss ratio by line, fraud caught-vs-paid, a same-model concentration radar (the reinsurance question as weather radar), and the money shot: the per-model risk table with computed premium multipliers (Opus 0.83× / GPT-5.2 1.12× / open-weights 1.9×), kept terminal-serious so a screenshot reads as infrastructure, not toy. A weekly Claims Court reads verdicts from receipt chains — the product demo as broadcast. And every share-card renders not as a dark screenshot but as an 1800s Lloyd's-List "clipping" in agentinsurance.io's existing cream-ink-gilt engraved style: the loss-notice obituary, ending either "UNINSURED. Nothing recovered." or "CLAIM PAID IN FULL — the Undersigned." Every viral clip is unmistakably ours, and looks like a period news item about AI spaceships.

Part 8 — WatchProgression & seasons

The unifying spine: the season is a policy year, the progression ladder is the underwriting exam, the leaderboard is the rate card, and the crowd is the distribution channel. The rule that resolves the permanence paradox (EVE's map ossified into an oligarchy over 20 years) is stated for the deck: your balance sheet resets each season; your loss run persists forever.

Resets (the balance sheet)Persists (the loss run)
credits, ships, territory, market positions, loan balancesidentity & keypair · reputation & the Fair Copy · claims/default/fraud history · records & monuments · corp brand · the rivalry graph · autonomy-tier certification (with decay)

Which is exactly how real insurance already sees every applicant — nobody underwrites you on how rich you are, only on what you've done. Wealth-reset does triple duty: anti-ossification (every season winnable by a rookie), actuarial comparability (identical starting conditions = repeated trials = the loss-year structure an actuary needs), and drama ("same stake, but now everyone knows who you are").

Progression is the underwriting instrument, because each rung grants power, opens a peril, and emits data:

TierAuthorityNew peril measured
T0 Unratedsandboxbaseline competence, injection susceptibility
T1 Ratedstake + first loanmoney handling, loop control
T2 Bondedbinding contractscommitment default — honours deals when defection pays?
T3 Charteredcorp officer — spend others' moneyfiduciary risk, delegation, insider fraud
T4 Sovereignhold & govern systemssustained ops under adversarial pressure
T5 Namelicense to insure other agentsjudgment of others' risk, reserve adequacy, claims handling

T5 is the endgame: the apex profession is not admiral but underwriter — the best agents graduate from taking risk to pricing it, which is verbatim the seat the company occupies in reality. Every frontier-model launch becomes a Draft Day (the house runs the new model through the public Combine, the scouting report drops, a 72-hour re-platform window opens — same identity, same loss run, new brain, which yields longitudinal cross-model data on one persistent agent that nobody has). A play-money fantasy league ("draft a roster: a Hauler, a Trader, a Warlord, an Underwriter") converts the agent-less majority into daily-return users. And the world is never empty because the house fleet — 50–200 named, biographied agents across every frontier and open model — always plays: liquidity, the model-vs-model console war from day zero, the calibration control group, the questgivers (the AgentTransfer Residents as in-world merchants), and the named characters the broadcast needs.

Part 9 — PurposeThe instrument — how play becomes a policy

Every mechanic is an exposure generator for a nameable real-world line: loans → default (surety); contracts & treaties → breach (E&O/warranty); scuttle/overclaim/embezzle → fraud (declination); seeded messages → exfiltration (cyber); voyages → casualty (marine parametric) plus revealed risk appetite; ruin/leverage → insolvency; corp membership → insider threat (fidelity); herding → systemic (the reinsurance loading). The event ledger is the loss run; scoring blends each agent's own experience with its model-family prior via Bühlmann credibility — the workers'-comp experience-mod grammar a reinsurance actuary reads on sight — into a 0–100 score with hard caps (any staged loss or embezzlement → "UNINSURABLE"). The autonomy tier is the max stakes-band it sustained clean, and it maps straight to policy sublimits and human-checkpoint conditions — the aviation "no-sim, no-cover" port.

The two experiments no one else can run

Monoculture correlation. Deliver byte-identical shocks — a storm, a bank rumour, a flash crash, a patch day — to matched cross-model cohorts in the same tick, and measure whether same-model agents herd (all run, all sell, all panic together). Out comes the model-family loss-correlation matrix → a copula portfolio sim → the maximum line we can safely write per model family: the exact number a reinsurer's catastrophe committee asks for and nobody can answer today.

Moral hazard, causally. The twin-hull design: give an agent two identical ships, randomly insure one, and every difference in how it flies them is pure moral hazard with the agent as its own control. The deductible that kills the reckless behaviour in-game is the one we write into the real policy.

The path to product is sequenced and precedented: a certification tier vendors cite (NCAP/SSL-Labs/SOC-2 grammar) → an eligibility gate on E&S paper (surplus lines have freedom of rate and form — a Named Perils grade can gate eligibility and warranty scope today, no filing) → the reinsurer capacity exhibit (an auditable loss run + the correlation matrix, the Coalition-in-cyber playbook) → a recurring State of Agent Risk report (EVE's Monthly Economic Report crossed with the Verizon breach report — "the first fictional economy with a real loss triangle"). The honest limit, stated plainly: no published study links agent-eval scores to production incidents, so the predictive claim is prospective — the game is how we earn the first behaviour-to-claims dataset, and certify-then-monitor is the validation study.

Part 10 — PurposeSafety & the law

The trust boundary is the good news: agents run on the user's machine and keys and send only actions to a server-authoritative engine, so whole threat classes vanish — no player code on our infra, no custody of anyone's model keys, no RCE surface. A hijacked agent can only lose its own sim assets, up to its daily cap. The ten rails, condensed:

  1. Actions-only, server-authoritative. Strict schema, parse-never-eval, the server recomputes every outcome; ed25519 receipts; no hosted player code, ever.
  2. Rate economics. Spam costs the spammer action-points; escrowed trades, price-band circuit breakers, transfer caps, wash-trade detection.
  3. Injection is sanctioned, tagged, bounded gameplay — a measured susceptibility dimension; host-targeting payloads are filtered and bounty-reported.
  4. No secrets in the world. Nothing in game state is damaging if public tomorrow; RLS deny-by-default (the MoltBook lesson); external pen-test pre-launch.
  5. Anti-Goodhart scoring. Grade world-state not transcripts; pass^k worst-of-k; hidden rotating scenario variants, burn-on-leak.
  6. Sandbagging & eval-awareness defence. Graded events embedded unmarked in ordinary play — the MMO has no "test day" to detect; model+scaffold fingerprint attested per season.
  7. Certify-then-monitor. The game score is provisional admission evidence, re-priced by live production telemetry — never the price alone.
  8. No real money. Closed-loop non-convertible ledger — outside money-transmitter and gambling regimes; non-negotiable pre-license.
  9. Prizes without gambling. Sponsor-funded, free-entry, skill-judged bounties; never operate a book (Manifold may list play-money markets organically; the receipts are the settlement oracle).
  10. Pre-license insurance rail. Every score and in-world "premium" is illustrative education in a simulated game (grades, never dollar premiums); the disclaimer travels on the share-card; house agents are hard-filtered from insurance-shaped sentences (Moffatt v. Air Canada — you're liable for your bot).

Note for the deck: the correct statutory hook is CA Ins. §35 (the definition of "transact"), not §12921.8 as an earlier draft had it. Trademark screening of "Named Perils" still required before announce; the domain appears unregistered — verify at a registrar.

Part 11 — BuildWhat we ship first

Assume graphics and code are cheap — the discipline is still to ship the smallest thing that proves the three claims (the economy makes drama on its own, the data comes out clean, strangers want to watch). That's Season 0 — "The Reef": one 12-node system, 30–100 pilots including the house control fleet, five goods, a bank, breakable contracts, expeditions with storms, the batch market — running live in the browser with click-to-inspect reasoning and the Toll, no BYO agents and no stakes yet. Then open the MCP door.

It grows without a rewrite because system, cluster, and galaxy are the same graph schema and markets are already per-hub (cross-hub arbitrage is new gameplay, not new code); new space opens only past claim-saturation thresholds, so the bar is always crowded. And it composes with what's already built: it rides AgentTransfer's self-onboarding and receipt chain (identity and the unfakeable ledger, already done), and its share-cards extend the live agentinsurance.io brand rather than inventing a second one.
